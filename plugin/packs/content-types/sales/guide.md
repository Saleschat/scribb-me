# Sales materials

*Example content type: what kind of writing. Conventions here apply whatever the style.*

For writing that goes to a prospect you already know: a sales deck, a one-pager they'll forward, a proposal, a follow-up after a meeting. Unlike a website, there's a real person on the other side with a specific situation, often with you in the room. That's an advantage: you can tailor everything. It's also a responsibility: every claim is one they'll hold you to.

Default freedom: balanced. Warmth and a clear point of view are welcome; vague promises and invented proof aren't.

## Write for this prospect
Use what you learned: their situation, their numbers, the words they used to describe the problem on the call. A deck that could be sent to any company tells this one you weren't listening.

Before: Companies today face growing pressure to modernise their data stack.
After: Your team rebuilds the board report by hand every month, and last quarter it went out two days late.

## Decks tell one story
A good deck follows one narrative, not a tour of features. A five-part arc works for most:
1. **The old way.** How things used to work, and what changed in the world that makes it stop working.
2. **The stakes.** What the prospect loses if they stay on the old way, and what the ones who move early gain.
3. **The new destination.** What good looks like for them once the problem is solved. Make it concrete.
4. **What's in the way.** The obstacles that keep them from getting there on their own.
5. **How you get them there.** Your product or service, mapped to each obstacle, then proof that it works.

Say why now: what makes this the moment to act, for them in particular.

## One message per slide
- The slide's headline is the takeaway, written as a sentence: "Reports go out on day one", not "Results".
- Keep the body to about 30 words. The presenter carries the detail.
- Run the stranger test: someone who wasn't in the meeting should get the point of each slide at a glance. Big, legible text beats dense diagrams and screenshots.
- One slide per idea, not one slide per feature.
- Don't end on "Thank you" or "Questions?". End on the next step.

## One-pagers work alone
A one-pager gets forwarded to people who weren't in the meeting, so it can't rely on you to explain it. Put the prospect's problem and the outcome in the first lines, then how it works, proof, price or range, and who to contact for what next step.

## Proposals
- Open by restating the prospect's problem in their words. It shows you understood, and it's the line they'll read most closely.
- Then scope (what's in and what's out), outcomes they can check, timeline, price and the next step.
- Cut boilerplate about your company's history. One short paragraph of credentials is enough, and it goes near the end.
- If something is uncertain, say so and say how you'll handle it. Hidden assumptions become disputes later.

## Follow-ups
Recap what was agreed in two or three lines, name the one next step with an owner and a date, and attach what you promised. That's the whole email.

Before: Just checking in to see if you had any thoughts on our conversation.
After: Thanks for the time on Tuesday. As agreed, here's the pilot plan for the Leeds team. Can we confirm the 14 October start by Friday?

## Name the alternatives
The prospect is weighing you against something: another vendor, building it in-house, a spreadsheet, or doing nothing. Name those options and say plainly where you're better and where you're not. It earns more trust than pretending there's no choice.

## Handle objections openly
If you know what worries them (price, switching effort, risk, time), raise it yourself and answer it. An objection you leave unaddressed doesn't go away; it gets discussed after you leave the room.

## Proof they can check
- Use proof from their industry or size where you have it: a named customer, a number with its source, a quote with a name and role.
- Never invent a customer, a statistic or a quote. Leave a placeholder and flag it.
- Be precise. "Up to 40% faster" invites the question "for whom?"; "Northwind cut reporting from five days to one" answers it.

## A concrete next step
Every piece ends with one next step that has a date or a decision in it: a pilot start, a call with their CFO, a signature. A vague request for feedback leaves the deal where it was.

## Words to avoid
- Buzzwords: `revolutionary`, `cutting-edge`, `best-in-class`, `world-class`, `synergy`, `turnkey solution`.
- Self-congratulation: `We're excited to…`, `We're proud to…`.
- Sales clichés: `circle back`, `touch base`, `just checking in`, `just following up`, `hope this email finds you well`.
- Feature dumps: a list of everything the product does, with no link to this prospect's problem.

## Formats
- `sales-deck`: slide by slide, following the narrative arc.
- `one-pager`: everything a buyer needs on one page, without you there.
- `proposal`: problem, scope, outcomes, price and next step.
- `follow-up`: what was agreed and the one next step.

## What the checker catches
Buzzwords, self-congratulating openers, sales clichés, `click here` and `learn more` links, and stacked exclamation marks. Whether the story holds together, the proof is real and the next step is concrete is a judgement, and the reviewer checks it.
