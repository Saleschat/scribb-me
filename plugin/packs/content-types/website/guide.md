# Website pages

*Example content type: what kind of writing. Conventions here apply whatever the style.*

For the public pages of a website: a landing page, a feature page, a pricing page, an about page, a case study. The reader is a stranger. Nobody is there to explain, and they'll leave in seconds if the page doesn't tell them what this is and why it matters to them. Decks, one-pagers, proposals and follow-ups for a known prospect are a different job; they belong to the Sales materials content type.

Default freedom: expressive. A page can have personality, humour and a point of view. It can't have hype, invented proof or claims it doesn't back up.

## Say what you sell, first
Within the first two lines, the reader has to know what the product is. The benefit can be the headline and the description the subheadline, or the other way round, but a clever slogan that needs decoding loses people before they get to the good part.

Before: Work, reimagined.
After: Invoicing for freelance designers. Get paid in days, not weeks.

## Open with their tension
Before the product, describe the moment the reader is stuck in, the way they'd describe it to a friend. If they recognise themselves, they'll read on. This section is easy to skip when you're excited about the product; don't skip it.

Before: Ledgerly is an AI-powered reconciliation platform for modern finance teams.
After: It's the third of the month and you're still matching receipts to transactions by hand.

## Use the customer's words
The best phrases on a page are usually ones a customer said first. Read reviews, support tickets and sales-call notes, and borrow how people describe the problem and the result. Your internal category name and your engineering terms mean nothing to a stranger.

## One idea per page
Decide the single thing the reader should believe when they leave, and build the page around it. A page that sells every feature to every reader sells nothing. If you have two ideas, you have two pages.

## Make one specific promise
State what changes for the reader, concretely. "Saves time" is a wish; "month-end goes from ten days to two" is a promise. Everything after it should support it.

## Name the alternative
The reader is already doing something about the problem: another tool, a spreadsheet, an agency, or nothing. Say what that is and why you're the better choice. It's how a stranger works out where you fit, and it's more honest than pretending you have no competition.

Before: The smarter way to manage invoices.
After: Still sending invoices from a spreadsheet template? Tally drafts them from your time logs and chases late payers for you.

## Answer the main objection early
Most readers arrive with one doubt: price, switching cost, trust, "will it work for someone like me". Find it by asking customers what almost stopped them, and answer it in the subheadline or the first section, not in an FAQ at the bottom.

## Back it with proof
- Use numbers you can stand behind, customers who agreed to be named, and real quotes with a name and a role.
- Never invent a statistic, a customer or a quote. Leave a placeholder and flag it.
- One strong, specific piece of proof beats five vague ones. "Trusted by thousands of teams" proves nothing; "Northwind closes its books in two days" proves something.
- Put the strongest proof near the top, where people decide whether to keep reading.

## Say who it's for, and who it isn't
Naming who the product isn't for makes the right reader trust the page more. "For finance teams of 2 to 20 people. If you run SAP, this isn't for you."

## Benefits before features
Every feature should answer "so what?" Lead with what the reader can now do, then the feature that makes it possible.

Before: Automatic bank-feed matching with configurable rules.
After: Your books match your bank every morning. Ledgerly matches each transaction overnight, using rules you set once.

## One call to action
- Give the page one main action. Start it with a verb and let it finish the page's story: "Start your free trial", "Book a 20-minute demo", "Get the price list".
- `Learn more` on its own tells the reader nothing. Say what they'll learn or get.
- A secondary action is fine if it's clearly secondary, such as "or see pricing".
- No fake urgency: no countdowns that reset, no "only 3 spots left" unless it's true.

## Sound like a person
Write the way the founder would explain the product across a table. Use "you" and "we", and contractions. Take a position; a page that hedges every claim persuades nobody. A brochure voice ("our solution empowers organizations to…") makes every company sound the same, which is the opposite of what a website is for.

## Make each line lead to the next
Read the page top to bottom and ask, after each line, whether the reader wants the next one. Cut lines that only restate the one before. Simple writing also exposes a thin idea; if a section is hard to say plainly, the thinking behind it may need work.

## Words to avoid
These say nothing a reader can check, so they slide past:
- Buzzwords: `revolutionary`, `cutting-edge`, `best-in-class`, `world-class`, `next-generation`, `industry-leading`, `seamless`, `empower`, `synergy`, `paradigm shift`, `turnkey solution`.
- Vague superlatives: `the best`, `the ultimate`, `#1`, unless you name who said so and where.
- Self-congratulation: `We're excited to…`, `We're proud to…`. The reader cares what's in it for them.
- Stacked adjectives: `a powerful, intuitive, flexible platform`. Pick the one that matters and prove it.
- The same claim three ways in a row. Say it once, then show it.

Before: We're thrilled to introduce our revolutionary, best-in-class platform that empowers teams to work smarter.
After: Ledgerly matches your bank feeds to your books every night. Month-end takes a short review.

## Layout
- Short paragraphs: one to three sentences.
- Section headings that state a benefit, so someone scanning only the headings still gets the story.
- Put the call to action where the reader decides: after the promise, and again at the end of a long page.
- Cut anything the reader doesn't need to decide. A page usually gets better when you remove the section you were least sure about.

## Formats
- `landing-page`: one product or offer, one action.
- `feature-page`: one feature and the job it does.
- `pricing-page`: plans, what each includes, and who each is for.
- `about-page`: who you are and why you built this.
- `case-study`: one customer's problem, what changed and the result.

## What the checker catches
Buzzwords, self-congratulating openers, `click here` and `learn more` links, and stacked exclamation marks. Whether the page says what you sell, opens with real tension, answers the objection and backs its promise is a judgement, and the reviewer checks it.
