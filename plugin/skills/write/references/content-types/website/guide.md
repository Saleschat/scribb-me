# Website and sales pages

*Example content type: what kind of writing. Conventions here apply whatever the style.*

For pages that have to win someone over: a landing page, a feature page, a pricing page, an about page, a case study, a sales one-pager, a sales deck. The reader didn't ask to be here and will leave in seconds if the page doesn't tell them why they should stay.

Default freedom: expressive. A page can have personality, humour and a point of view. It can't have hype, invented proof or claims it doesn't back up.

## One idea per page
Decide the single thing the reader should believe when they leave, and build the page around it. A page that tries to sell every feature to every reader sells nothing. If you have two ideas, you have two pages.

## The headline
- Say what the reader gets, in words they'd use. "Close the month in two days" beats "The future of finance operations".
- Be specific. A number, a time, a result.
- Skip clever wordplay that needs a second read. The subheadline can add detail; the headline has to work alone.

Before: Reimagining how teams work together.
After: Run your weekly team meeting in 15 minutes.

## Start with their problem
Describe the problem the way the reader would describe it to a friend, before you mention the product. If they nod at the problem, they'll read the solution. Use their words, not your category name.

Before: Ledgerly is an AI-powered reconciliation platform for modern finance teams.
After: You spend the first week of every month matching receipts to transactions by hand.

## Make one specific promise
State what changes for the reader, concretely. "Saves time" is a wish; "cuts month-end from ten days to two" is a promise. Then keep it: everything after the promise should support it.

## Back it with proof
- Use numbers you can stand behind, named customers who agreed to be named, and real quotes with a name and a role.
- Never invent a statistic, a customer or a quote. Leave a placeholder and flag it instead.
- One strong piece of proof beats five vague ones. "Trusted by thousands of teams" proves nothing; "Northwind closes its books in two days" proves something.

## Say who it's for, and who it isn't
Naming who the product isn't for makes the right reader trust the page more. "For finance teams of 2 to 20 people. If you run SAP, this isn't for you."

## Benefits before features
Every feature on the page should answer "so what?" Lead with what the reader can now do, then the feature that makes it possible.

Before: Automatic bank-feed matching with configurable rules.
After: Your books match your bank every morning. Ledgerly matches each transaction overnight, using rules you set once.

## One call to action
- Give the page one main action, and say what happens when they take it: "Start a 14-day trial", "Book a 20-minute demo", "Download the price list".
- `Learn more` on its own tells the reader nothing. Say what they'll learn or get.
- A secondary action is fine if it's clearly secondary, such as "or see pricing".
- No fake urgency: no countdowns that reset, no "only 3 spots left" unless it's true.

## Sound like a person
Write the way the founder would explain the product across a table. Use "you" and "we". Contractions are fine. A brochure voice ("our solution empowers organizations to…") makes every company sound the same, which is the opposite of what a website is for.

## Words to avoid
These say nothing a reader can check, so they slide past:
- Buzzwords: `revolutionary`, `cutting-edge`, `best-in-class`, `world-class`, `next-generation`, `industry-leading`, `seamless`, `empower`, `synergy`, `paradigm shift`, `turnkey solution`.
- Vague superlatives: `the best`, `the ultimate`, `#1`, unless you name who said so and where.
- Self-congratulation: `We're excited to…`, `We're proud to…`. The reader cares what's in it for them.
- Stacked adjectives: `a powerful, intuitive, flexible platform`. Pick the one that matters and prove it.
- The same claim three ways in a row. Say it once, then show it.

Before: We're thrilled to introduce our revolutionary, best-in-class platform that empowers teams to work smarter.
After: Ledgerly matches your bank feeds to your books every night. Month-end takes a short review.

## Layout
- Short paragraphs: one to three sentences.
- Section headings that state a benefit, so someone scanning only the headings still gets the story.
- Put the call to action where the reader decides: after the promise, and again at the end of a long page.
- Cut anything the reader doesn't need to decide. A page gets better when you remove the section you were least sure about.

## Sales decks
- One message per slide, and the slide's headline states that message as a sentence: "Teams close the month in two days", not "Results".
- About 30 words of body per slide at most. The speaker carries the detail.
- The order follows the reader's decision: their problem, what changes, proof, how it works, what it costs, the next step.
- A slide that only says "Thank you" or "Questions?" is a wasted slide. End on the next step.

## Formats
- `landing-page`: one product or offer, one action.
- `feature-page`: one feature and the job it does.
- `pricing-page`: plans, what each includes, and who each is for.
- `about-page`: who you are and why you built this.
- `case-study`: one customer's problem, what changed and the result.
- `sales-one-pager`: everything a buyer needs on one page.
- `sales-deck`: slide by slide, one message each.

## What the checker catches
Buzzwords, self-congratulating openers, `click here` and `learn more` links, and stacked exclamation marks. Whether the page has one idea, real proof and a clear action is a judgement, and the reviewer checks it.
