# Narrative essay

*Style: who it sounds like. Taste layered on top of a content type. The content type wins on a conflict. For newsletters, pair it with the `essay` format, which carries the structure this voice needs.*

A style for founder-written newsletter issues that read like a story rather than a stack of headed sections. It sounds like someone who was there, telling you what happened and what they now think it means. The reader should want the next paragraph because the story isn't finished, not because a heading promised something.

Good for: newsletters (email), in particular essay issues, launch stories and "what we learned" issues. It can also tell a product update as a short story. For a digest of several unrelated items, use another style or none.

## Voice
- First person. "I" for what you saw, did and believe. "We" for the company or for everyone. "You" when you speak to the reader directly, now and then rather than in every paragraph.
- Give judgements as yours: "I think", "I suspect", "my guess is". Use them for opinions only, never for facts you can check.
- Admit things. Saying what you got wrong, what you doubted or what it cost you does more for trust than any adjective.
- Conversational connectors: "But", "So", "Still", "And yet". Asides go in parentheses.

## The opening
Open with one of these three:
- **A scene.** A person, a moment, a date and a place, told in the past tense. *"At 6:40 on a Saturday, the baker on our street had already sold out of the loaf she'd spent two days making."*
- **An admission.** *"I was sure customers wanted more settings. I was wrong, and it took me eight months to notice."*
- **One plain claim** that the rest of the issue earns.

The scene is about the news, not a warm-up before it. Keep it to three sentences or fewer, and say the point in plain words by the end of the second paragraph.

## The arc
A typical issue moves through five steps:
1. **Scene or admission.**
2. **The turn.** Show why the moment matters beyond itself. *"That's the version of the story everyone tells. It leaves out the oven."*
3. **The claim**, in one or two sentences.
4. **The evidence**: a second episode, a number, a short history. Each piece comes as story first, meaning second.
5. **The complication**: the strongest reason you might be wrong, taken seriously.

Then go back to the scene or to your own stake, and stop.

Mark a turn with a short paragraph: a fact on its own line, a one-word reply, or a question you actually answer next. Don't stack rhetorical questions.

## Moving between story and argument
- Story first, meaning after. Tell the episode with its concrete details, then interpret it in one or two short sentences. Never explain the point and then illustrate it.
- Let a story paragraph run long when it is one scene. Keep an interpretation paragraph short.
- Use quotes only when a person in the story says something. Keep them to a sentence or two and attribute them in the line: *"We'll fix it on Monday," the landlord told her, three Mondays in a row.*

## Paragraphs and sentences
- Paragraphs range from one line to about 120 words. About a third can be one sentence. Use very short paragraphs, under six words, two or three times per issue at most.
- Sentences vary widely. Let a long one carry a scene with its clauses, then follow it with a short one: *"She closed the shop at four, drove the van across town herself, and still made the delivery before the client's guests arrived. Nobody asked her to."*
- A one-line paragraph states a fact or a turn. It must not be a moral ("And that's what leadership is."). The base pack flags tidy morals, and they are where this voice tips into cliché.

## Headings and lists
- A single-story issue has no headings. A longer issue that really changes subject halfway can have one or two plain ones.
- No summary list at the top ("In this issue:"). The opening does that job.
- Use a numbered list only for things that really are a list: three reasons, five steps. Never use bullets to carry the argument.

## Numbers and sources
- Be exact: "January 14", "$3,200", "41 customers", rather than "recently", "a lot", "many".
- Give a number with something to compare it to: *"Refunds went from 4% in March to just under 2% in June, which is still twice what I'd like."* "About one in five" reads better than "21.4%" unless the decimal matters.
- Name the source in the sentence and link it: "a survey of 300 shop owners by X found…", "per the company's filing". Don't write "studies show".
- Put disclosures (you invested, you were paid, you got early access) in a footnote or a P.S., not halfway through the story.

## The ending
- End on a callback to the opening scene, on what happens next, or on an honest open question. *"She still bakes it on Saturdays. She just makes forty now."*
- Don't summarise and don't add an aphorism. If the last paragraph restates the argument, cut it.
- Then a short, consistent sign-off with a real name. One line about what's coming next is fine.

## Words
- Favour plain verbs and concrete nouns, and the reader's own words for things.
- Avoid report connectors ("Furthermore", "Moreover", "In conclusion"), signposts ("In this issue", "Key takeaways", "Let's get into it") and hype. Excitement shows in the details, not in adjectives.
- Avoid profanity and in-group slang. A founder newsletter reaches customers.

## Before and after
Before:
> ## What changed
> Support hours are now 7am–7pm.
> ## Why
> Customer feedback showed demand for earlier support.

After:
> Last Tuesday at 6:52 in the morning, a café owner sent us a message about a broken card reader. She had a queue out the door. We answered at nine.
>
> From Monday, someone answers from seven.

## Checker rules
`NarrativeEssay.ReportSignposts` flags openers that frame an issue as a report. `NarrativeEssay.EssayConnectors` suggests plainer connectors for "furthermore", "moreover" and similar words. The rest of this style (the arc, the scene, the paragraph rhythm) needs judgement, so the reviewer checks it.
